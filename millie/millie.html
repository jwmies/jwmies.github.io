Ontario Provincial Parks, Fall 2026

Summary

The Provincial Parks of Ontario were beautiful and, despite the current affairs between the US and Canada (tariffs, etc.), the Canadians we met were wonderful; all were friendly and welcoming. To pick a favorite among the parks would be difficult; they were all so nice.

Border crossings were trivial. There were no inspections of our vehicle or our camper. Going into Canada, questions focused on firearms and coming back into the states, we were asked if we had "anything with bark on it". Having neither, we went on our way in both directions.

En route to and from Ontario, we enjoyed our stays in Michigan, much as we did on our 2024 trip. Of the Michigan State Parks, Tahquamenon Falls remains our favorite.

We monitored our consumptions of water, propane, and 12V electricity to refine our previous determinations. To give this some context, we had 30A shore power for 27 of the 43 nights (44 days) we were out and 15A shore power for 6 nights, which is luxurious for us. (More commonly, we're off grid, without connections.) We only had a city water connection for 2 of our 43 nights. For hot water, we ran the heater on propane for 10 to 15 minutes each day and cooked with propane for 14 days. (We used an electric hot plate for cooking when we had 30A shore power.) We did not run the furnace at all; instead, we used our ceramic heater when it got chilly. With this, our daily consumptions were 6 gallons of water, 8Ah of 12V electricity (provided by a 140Ah LiFePO4 battery), and a little less than 0.5 lb of propane. We typically consumed an additional 1 to 2 gallons of water from refilled water bottles per day for coffee, drinking, and other cooking. It should be noted that our battery was used for interior lights (2 to 3 hours), radio and antenna booster (1 to 2 hours), bathroom fan (1 hour), water pump, refrigerator electronics and sparker, hot water heater electronics and sparker, thermostat, CO/propane detector, and occasional use of slide motors, tongue jack, and awning motor and that we powered our television and recharged our devices with a 300W Jackery. It should also be noted that we only recharge our battery with our 100W suitcase solar panels and a 20A lithium battery charger; we leave leave the breaker for our converter off.

Lake Macatawa Campground, Holland State Park, Holland, MI
10/06-10/10/2026
Site 147
Connections: 30A electric; water spigot near site; dump station

At first, Holland was a bit of a disappointment, mostly due to seasonal closures. Once at the campground, we also regretted being in the Woodstock loop; sites in the Pines loop are much nicer and the bathhouse was recently rebuilt and is remarkably nice. (We heard that a rebuild is scheduled for the Woodstock loop bathhouse.) Despite our initial impressions, we came to thoroughly enjoy Holland.

Our site (#147) was spacious and more private than most in the Woodstock loop, though it was close to Ottawa Beach Road.

From the campground, we walked to Black Lake Boardwalk and Ottawa Beach, to the Reservoir Grill for breakfast one morning, and climbed Mount Pisgah (239 steps), which is a particularly tall, vegetated sand dune with great views of the area.

We discovered that downtown Holland is very nice. A stroll on 8th street included a visit to Kilwins for ice cream. Heidi shopped at Field's Fabrics to support her quilting projects. The Holland Museum was well worth the visit, particular for its displays of Dutch art. Any visit to the museum should include a walk through Centennial Park, which is beautiful, even at the time of our visit (no tulips).

We really enjoyed Windmill Island, particularly our tour of the windmill.

Deboer Bakkerij (bakery and restaurant) is deserving of special mention. We went there for breakfast one morning. Oma's Way omelet (asparagus, portabella mushroom, sun-dried tomatoes, artichoke hearts, and Boursin cheese) was the best!

Petoskey State Park, Petoskey, MI
10/02-10/06/2026
Site 165
Connections: 30A electric; water spigot near site; dump station


Our site (#165) was at the end of the last loop in the Tannery Creek Campground, directly across from a short boardwalk to the beach. This loop of the campground is less wooded than others and many of the sites, including ours, provide 50A power. Consequently, our little RPod was dwarfed by very large RVs. Our site was smaller than most, barely long enough to accommodate our truck and trailer, and was on the inside of a tight curve at the end of the loop. This made it a bit awkward to back in to. We were also careful to provide plenty of room for long rigs to make the turn. Regardless, it turned out to be a nice site.



Our proximity to the beach was convenient for hunting Petoskey Stones and for catching the beautiful sunsets that occurred every evening. With an onshore wind gusting to 35 mph, the surf was also very impressive at times.



We enjoyed Petoskey, with visits to Bayfront Park, to the downtown area during an Octoberfest, hosted in part by Beards Brewery (burgers and beers), and the Petoskey Brewing Company (more burgers and beers). For some of the essentials (groceries and laundry), we visited D&W Fresh Market and the Bayview Laundromat. We didn't make it to Kilwins (ice cream and fudge), at least not in Petoskey.


From Petoskey, we drove to Charlevoix one morning. We had hoped for breakfast at That French Place, but found it closed. Instead, we went to Harwood Gold Store & Cafe, which suited us well for a light breakfast and a little gift shopping.

Grundy Lake Provincial Park, Britt, Ontario
09/28-09/30/2026
Site 339
Connections: 30A electric (20A circuit w/15A cord); water spigot near site; dump station


The campground at Grundy Lake was particularly quiet and peaceful and campers were very friendly, easy to talk to. Our site (#339) was spacious, but circumstances of the site were like those at Presqu'ile, which limited our shore power to 15A.


We didn't kayak, as we only had one full day. Instead, we took in the near-peak foliage on a hike to Pakeshkap (Pak) Lake. We also enjoyed the beaches on Gurd Lake and Pak Lake.


There's quite a campaign to protect turtle nests here. The nests are everywhere, in the dirt roads, in campsites, and along trails. We reported one that we found on our hike to Pak Lake; it had been partially scavenged by animals.



A funny thing happened here. Folks at the R-Pod Rally (Presqu'ile) left us with their surplus firewood, much more than we would normally have, which we brought with us to Grundy Lake. Then, while we were on our hike, someone left us with a load of firewood and even stacked it nicely by our fire pit. The humor comes in the fact that (1) we seldom have a campfire, (2) at this point, we had more than we could possibly burn, and (3) we were crossing back into the US the next day and we couldn't bring it with us. Indeed, we were asked at the crossing if we "had anything with bark on it"; we didn't; we had left our surplus firewood, lots of it, at our campsite.

Presqu'ile Provincial Park, Brighton, Ontario
09/24-09/28/2026
Site 58
Connections: 30A electric (20A circuit w/15A cord); water spigot near site; dump station


Our campsite (#58) was on the inside of the loop directly across from access to a small south-facing beach with views of High Bluff Island and Gull Island. Due to circumstances of the site, namely the distance to a shared pedestal, which happened to be on the door side of the trailer, we were forced to use both our 15A and our 30A cords (50 feet total) and to plug in to a 20A circuit. The circuit and cords tolerated our 12A ceramic heater just fine.


The beach across from our site provided a perfect spot for morning coffee, for Heidi to sit with a book, and to watch 2 Mute Swans that hung around for our entire stay.


The sunny (warm) side of the trailer and the truck drew lots of Stable Flies, which were a bit of a nuisance; their bites, even through clothing, are painful.


We had a great time meeting and sharing stories with folks at the Ontario R-Pod Rally. In fact, we hosted the group at our site both Friday and Saturday nights, as ours was best suited.


The marsh boardwalk was exceptional. We also walked to the lighthouse, where we learned about shipping and rum-running on Lake Ontario.


Brighton, only several miles away, was a great town for shopping, laundry, etc.


We also made a trip to the Big Apple for apple bread and apple pie.

Canisbay Lake Campground, Algonquin Provincial Park, South Nipissing District, Ontario
09/19-09/24/2026
Site 106
Connections: 30A electric; water spigot near site; dump station (remarkable 4-lane dump station)


Algonquin is a spectacular park and is a contender for one of our favorites. It seems that we enjoyed it while it was less crowded. We missed the summer crowd and, although the foliage was near peak during our visit, that crowd came the next weekend, as reported on Facebook.


Our hikes included Spruce Bog, Beaver Pond, Two Rivers, part of the Mizzy Lake trail, and the Algonquin Logging Museum. Jon also hiked the Fork Lake Trail, Whiskey Rapids, where he saw a beaver, Hardwood Lookout, and Peck Lake.


The Visitor's Center is remarkable, with many amazing displays and a very good video (theater). We also enjoyed our visits to the Algonquin Art Center and the Logging Museum.


Our campsite at Canisbay Lake was nice, but had no sun, which we would have welcomed considering the chilly conditions (highs in the 50's and lows in the 30's, F). It was also a bit far from the beach where we put in our kayak, but we managed. Campsites in the Canadian Provincial Parks we visited shared electrical pedestals, placed half way between sites. At this site, we set up as close to the pedestal as possible and still barely reached it with our 25 ft 30A cord.


We paddled on Canisbay Lake twice, once to the far end of the lake and once through the shallow marshy waterway to the Cache Lake portage. From our kayak, we checked out several of the paddle-in campsites, some of which are very nice, and a beaver lodge near the portage. We also paddled with an otter that seemed curious about us.

Lighthouse B Campground, Killbear Provincial Park, Georgian Bay, Lake Huron, Ontario
09/14-09/19/2026
Site 1433
Connections: 30A electric; water spigot near site; dump station


Our pull-through site at Killbear, between two campground roads, was nice. It was a short walk to the bathhouse. As Heidi noted, the showers were "amazing". The put-in for the kayak was also a short walk down a campground road, an easy trek with the kayak strapped onto the dolly.


After 10 days without, having shore power at Killbear (and for the rest of our trip) was a luxury. We used it for our ceramic heater and our hot plate, and to recharge our lithium battery.


We paddled around Patterson Island, in Parry Sound, where we were greeted by a juvenile bald eagle. We had hoped to paddle in Blind Bay, but that part of the campground was closed (seasonal closure), which limited our access with the kayak.


We hiked to Lookout Point two times, once clockwise and once counterclockwise. This is a great hike, partially on boardwalks, to an overlook that provides views of Blind Bay and Patterson Island. We also hiked to Lighthouse point and adjacent beaches from our campsite.


The town of Parry Sound served us well one day for laundry, Timmy's (Tim Horton), pizza for lunch, gift shopping, and getting our leaking tire plugged (Ford dealership/service). It's a nice town, 30 minutes from the park.

Mississagi Provincial Park, 30 km North of Elliot Lake, Ontario
09/09-09/14/2026
Site 19
Connections: none; water spigot near site; dump station


The 1-hour drive up to Mississagi from Huron Shores, on ON rt. 546, was pretty barren. It's an unmarked road (no lines), paved but rough, with very few houses and even less traffic. We may have seen 2 or 3 cars the entire way. When we got to the campground, on a Wednesday afternoon, we were virtually the only ones there. Many seasonal sites had trailers, tents, boats, etc. on them, but owners were absent. There were no other regular campers. With no guidance, it was difficult to find our site and, when we did, we found it very challenging. It was an awkward pull-through and very unlevel (side-to-side), but we managed and it turned into a wonderful stay.


A small beach on Semiwhite Lake, perfect for a couple chairs, was accessed from our site down a 50-meter rocky trail. Sites 20-24 may have been more convenient and a bit closer to the lake, but our little beach couldn't be beat.




We thoroughly enjoyed a 7.5-km hike to an overlook of Helenbar Lake, where we watched groups of loons display territorial behavior with their different calls and their wing rowing.


Despite a strong breeze we made two paddling trips on Semiwhite Lake, which is completely undeveloped other than the campground beach. Rather than contend with getting the kayak down the trail from our campsite, we chose to use our dolly for the first time and put in at the campground beach. The dolly worked well, but without a strap, it shifted a few times under the kayak. (At Killbear, we used a strap.)


We also made two trips to the town of Elliot Lake, 30 km south of the park, where we learned of its mining history and came to enjoy Tim Hortons (Timmy's/Timmies).


A few other campers joined us by the weekend.

Rivermouth Pines (Rustic) Campground, Tahquamenon Falls State Park, Paradise, MI
09/04-09/09/2026
Sites 110 and 117
Connections: none; water spigot near site; dump station


We love Tahquamenon Falls State Park and prefer to camp in the rustic section of Rivermouth Pines. There are no connections in the rustic section, but the sites are pretty spectacular. We're also partial to site 110, which we had a few years ago and for the first few days of this stay. Site 117 was also very nice and was even more convenient for launching the kayak. It's a nice walk from the rustic sites, along the river, to bathroom and shower facilities in the modern section of the campground.


This place is perfect for kayaking. We kayaked both upstream and downstream, to where the river empties into Lake Superior. One evening, just before dark, Jon noticed a red canoe drifting into the river from the riverbank, just upstream from our site (117), with no one on board. Earlier in the day, he noticed it on the roof of a matching red Mazda SUV. He ran up the road and found the car but no one was at the site. He and another camper, Brian, commandeered one of the park's kayaks and used our paddles to rescue the canoe. The two of them were pretty proud of themselves and had a great time saving the day.


One morning, we woke up to a fog bow. It's a thing. From Wikipedia: A fog bow, sometimes called a white rainbow, is a phenomenon similar to a rainbow ...
We relived some of our experiences from a few years ago with a hike to upper falls and a beer (Tannin Red) and burger at the brewery.


It was also here that we started using our compressor to maintain air pressure in our front, passenger-side tire, which was losing close to 1psi per day. We looked for the leak with soapy water, but couldn't find it. (The puncture was plugged in Parry Sound, ON.)

Fall Creek Falls State Park, Spencer, TN
06/09-06/12/2026
Site 80 (Loop B)
Connections: water and electricity; dump station

We've camped at Fall Creek Falls State Park several times now and have developed a preference for Loop B. Our site this time (#80) was on a 7-site cul-de-sac, convenient to the bath house (but not too close), level, mostly shaded, and had connections for water and electricity. It was very nice.

Despite a little sleep deprivation (see below), we enjoyed our 3-day stay. We paddled 3 to 4 miles each day and did 1.5 kts using one of our "Houdini" wind breakers as a sail. We also hiked the 2-mile round trip to the Falls from the nature center. Meal highlights included pot roast and a cinnamon-roll Bundt cake from our RockPot, and one meal out at the Ugly Mug Bar & Grill, in Spencer, a 25-minute drive. The Ugly Mug was recommended by our son.

Launching a personal kayak at the boat dock costs $6. For campers, that's a 1-time fee for the duration of your stay and it's well worth it. We used the ADA compliant launch, which was really handy, disabled or not. On one of our paddles, we noticed that there's a similar launch at the northwest end of the dam, which may be free.

Loud conversations of several teenagers in the site next to us continued well into quiet hours, which made it difficult to sleep with the windows open. Fortunately, they left after our first night and their site was vacant for the rest of our stay. But to be honest, sleep was a bit of a struggle anyway. Our air conditioner is a bit too loud and the evenings were a bit too warm and humid to be comfortable without it. We were reminded of why we prefer to camp in the dry air and cool evenings of the west, where we commonly sleep under a lofty quilt and in complete silence, even in the summer.

The Park has renovated their main dump station, expanding it to 3 islands. I don't recall perfectly, but there may be 2 stations at each island, which is a huge improvement. However, each island is elevated, with a high curb, such that dumping from a low trailer, like an R•Pod, is very difficult. On our next visit, we'll dump at the old dump station in Loop E, assuming it's still there.

Some of our past visits to the Park have included our grandchildren, who thoroughly enjoyed swimming in Cane Creek, searching for gemstones in the sluice at the General Store, roasting marshmallows in the campfire, and riding their bikes around the campground.


Spring in the Blue Ridge of Southeast Tennessee and Southwest North Carolina

Standing Indian Campground, Franklin, NC
05/04-05/07/2026
Site 34
Connections: none; water spigot near site; dump station

Standing Indian, with its 84 sites, distributed in 5 loops, is a beautiful campground in Nantahala National Forest. Our site (#34), at the edge of a clearing in Loop 3, provides considerable sunshine (solar opportunity) in early May, before trees are completely leafed out. Several nice hiking trails lead out the campground and a trailhead for the AT is just a mile or so up the road. On a rainy morning, we drove to Franklin for breakfast at Ms. Lois' Restaurant, which was a perfect choice. We made a few visits to Poplar Cove Overlook, on Hwy 64, for cell service. The Episcopal Chapel and graveyard, on the road into the campground is worth checking out. At the time of our visit, Loop 5 (sites 55 to 84) was closed, apparently for site renovation (new timbers, gravel, etc.). Thinking about our next visit, he last several sites along the outside of this densely wooded loop are particularly nice and would accommodate our small trailer, if trailers are permitted.

Parksville Lake RV Campground, Benton, TN
05/01-05/04/2026
Site 2
Connections: electricity; water spigot near site; dump station

The RV campground is very nice; it's densely wooded with only 16 reasonably spaced, level sites, arranged in a single elongate loop, bounded by a creek on one side. (Sites 6, 7, and 9 are creekside; 7 and 9 are nice pull throughs.) The entrance is a bit obscure; don't miss it. We've stayed here twice before, once in site 1 and once in site 16; this time, we're in site 2, across from the hosts. The hosts for this visit, the Smiths, are very nice and take great care of the campground, which includes a single small building with separate heated restrooms for men and women, each with flush toilets and a single hot shower. The new Food City in Benton (jct. 64/411), with its Starbucks and 5G cell service, is a great grocery store, just 6 miles west of the campground. We intended to put our inflatable kayak into the lower Ocoee at the first designated access above Parksville Lake, but found it extremely crowded with avid white-water paddlers on a Sunday afternoon. Rather than face the embarrassment of our inflatable among all the high-tech white-water boats (and the wet suites, helmets, GoPros, etc.), we decided to return another day, midweek. Apparently, there's a small abandoned cemetery on the hill across the road (TN 30), south of the Group (tent-only) Campground, variously known as the Price Cemetery, Cloud Cemetery, or Rock Creek Cemetery, with graves as old as the mid 1800's (the cemetery detective). We tried to find it, among the dense undergrowth and blown down trees, without success. From here, we go to Standing Indian Campground, near Franklin, NC.

Indian Boundary Campground, Tellico Plains, TN
04/26-05/01/2026
Site 50B
Connections: electricity; water spigot near site; dump station

Left home at around noon to arrive at Indian Boundary around 2:30. The drive from Tellico Plains to the campground on Cherohala Skyway was an experience, to say the least, due to sharp curves and large groups of fast sports cars and motorcycles. Even mid-day of midweek, we encountered groups of 30 or more Porsches, Minis, Corvettes, Harley Davidsons, etc., paying no mind to the 40 mph speed limit. Indian Boundary is one of the nicest campgrounds we've been to in the southeast, considering our interests in hiking and kayaking. Every day, we hiked the trail around the lake (3.5 miles) and explored its shores in our kayak and, on each of these adventures, we discovered something new. There's not a bad site in the campground and ours (#50) is pretty typical, though there are a few in A loop with direct access to the lake. The campground also has a nice camp store with a great selection of ice cream. Although there's no cell service in the campground, we found minimal service at Turkey Creek Overlook, 5 miles up the Skyway, and excellent service in Tellico Plains (15 miles).



Our First Southeast Coastal Trip (Georgia—South Carolina—North Carolina)
Tanglewood Park Campground, Clemmons, NC
03/17-03/18/2026
Site C17
Connections: water, electricity and sewer (full hookups)

This was just an overnight stop for us, on our way home to Chattanooga from Oregon Inlet. Tanglewood is a very nice, wooded, rv-only campground. The sites are relatively large and nicely spaced, but some are not particularly level. Our site, at the edge of a large field, was nicer than most. Although this was just an overnight stop for us, we took advantage of the extensive walking trails in the park, which are easily accessed from the campground.

Oregon Inlet Campground, Cape Hatteras National Seashore, Nags Head, NC
03/13-03/17/2026
Site C17
Connections: water and electricity; dump station across Highway 12

Sites at Oregon Inlet are very narrow, are not particularly long, and are closely spaced. We had no problem fitting, but it was a bit of a struggle for larger rigs. The campground is a short walk, through the dunes, to the ocean-side beach, where we flew kites one afternoon. We spent our last night hooked up to our tow vehicle, as Millie shook in gusty 40+ mph winds. She performed perfectly, despite heavy wind-driven rain. We enjoyed our visit with friends, who camped in a nearby site. We went out with them to The Dunes (restaurant) for a breakfast and to Salt & Cypress Kitchen and Cocktails for a dinner, both of which were very nice. We also thoroughly enjoyed our visit to The Kitty Hawk First Flight Museum and the Wright Brothers Monument. Note that the dump station is not in the campground; instead, it's at the Oregon Inlet Fishing Center, on the other side of Highway 12.

Cedar Point Campground, Croatan National Forest, near Emerald Isle, NC
03/09-03/13 2026
Site 14
Connections: electricity only; water spigot near site; dump station

Sites at Cedar Point Campground are all pretty similar, paved and level with ample space between them; most are quite large (long), by our standards. The bathhouse is OK, but could stand some renovation. Showers, in individual shower rooms (a plus), are warm, if you wait long enough (several cycles of the spring-loaded push button). Found beach access, with free parking, near Indian Beach, on Bogue Banks (island), 13 miles from the campground. It's a short drive to Publix, just 10 minutes (4.5 miles), for groceries. Considered doing the Paddler's Loop at Cedar Point, but parts of it, viewed from Tidelands Trail, would not be navigable in our kayak. Time and weather constraints also worked against us for kayaking. Tidelands trail, some of which is on elevated boardwalk, is very nice and the trailhead is a short walk from the campground. Overall, this is a nice, quiet campground, very pleasant.

Little Pee Dee Sate Park, Dillon, SC
03/06-03/09 2026
Site 9
Connections: water and electricity; dump station

Had a beautiful lake-front site at Little Pee Dee State Park. Enjoyed kayaking, hiking, and relaxing. Be forewarned; it is a bit noisy, with honking (geese), quacking (ducks), peeping (frogs), and hooting (owls), all pleasant sounds. For supplies, Walmart, Food Lion, etc. are 20 minutes away, in Dillon. The sites are sand and the roads are dirt, but even with the rain we had on our last night, it wasn't bad. Thoroughly enjoyed our stay.

Skidaway Island State Park, Savannah, GA
03/02-03/06 2026
Site 65
Connections: water and electricity; dump station

Drove to Skidaway from home (Chattanooga). Left very early trying to avoid Atlanta traffic, but ended up in the midst of it. Arrived at Skidaway shortly after 1:00 pm to discover that site 65 is a handicapped site, with indicated restrictions. When we made our online reservations, all sites were represented as handicap accessible. We were assured by park staff that it was no problem for us to occupy it. Other than that, our pull-through site was perfect for our stay in this beautiful park. The bathhouses are very nice, each with 2 showers and laundry facilities; walking trails lead from the campground to an adjacent marsh; and the camp store is well worth visiting. At the time of our visit, major renovations to parts of the campground were underway, which were a bit of a distraction, but should benefit future visitors. During our three full days at Skidaway, we enjoyed downtown Savannah, Tybee Island, Fort Pulaski National Monument, and Wormsloe Historic Site, all of which were worthwhile. Heidi is only now reading Midnight in the Garden of Good and Evil and wishes she had done so before visiting Savannah.



Winterized
11/15/2025-03/01/2026

Millie took a long winter's nap after our Fall trip to the southwestern states (CO, AZ, UT, NV, CA, AZ, NM).
